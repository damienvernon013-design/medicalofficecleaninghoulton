In-House vs. Outsourced Medical Office Cleaning: The Real Cost Comparison

The visible hourly wage is rarely the full cost of keeping cleaning in-house.

The Visible Cost Isn't the Full Cost

Most practices calculate in-house cleaning cost as hourly wage times hours worked. That number misses a significant share of the real expense — and the gap is larger in a medical facility than in a standard office, because of the added training and compliance requirements.

Costs Practices Often Overlook

  • Payroll taxes and benefits: employer-side taxes, unemployment insurance, and any benefits add meaningfully on top of the base wage.
  • Bloodborne pathogen training: staff who clean clinical spaces need OSHA-required training on bloodborne pathogen exposure — a cost and a liability that doesn't apply to general office cleaning.
  • Turnover and retraining: cleaning staff turnover is high industry-wide, and every departure in a medical setting means retraining someone on infection-control protocols specific to your facility.
  • Supplies and equipment: EPA List N-registered disinfectants and the equipment to apply them correctly cost more than general-purpose cleaning products.
  • Supervision time: someone on staff has to manage scheduling, verify protocol compliance, and handle callouts — real management overhead that's easy to undercount.
  • Liability exposure: an in-house cleaning employee injured on the job affects your workers' comp rates directly. An outside provider's insurance covers their own staff.
  • Inconsistent coverage: when your in-house cleaner is out sick, exam rooms don't get disinfected on schedule. A commercial provider carries backup staff for exactly this reason.

When In-House Can Make Sense

For very large multi-location health systems with dedicated facilities staff, an in-house team can work. For most independent practices and small clinics, outsourcing to a provider trained specifically in clinical disinfection protocols removes the training burden and the coverage risk.
