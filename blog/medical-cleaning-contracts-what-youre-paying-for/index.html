Medical Cleaning Contracts: What You're Actually Paying For

The scope-of-work section determines what you receive. Here's what to check before signing.

The Scope of Work Section Is Everything

The most important part of any medical office cleaning contract is the scope of work. It should detail every task the crew performs, how often, and in which areas of your facility — exam rooms, operatories, waiting areas, restrooms, and back-office space. Vague language like "general cleaning" is a red flag in any commercial cleaning agreement, and it is a bigger problem in a medical facility, where the difference between routine cleaning and a documented disinfection protocol matters for compliance.

Line Items Specific to Medical and Dental Facilities

  • Exam table and high-touch surface disinfection between patients or at close of day
  • EPA List N-registered disinfectant use with documented dwell times
  • Dental operatory and sterilization-room-adjacent surface cleaning
  • Waiting room and reception high-touch disinfection frequency
  • Restroom sanitization and restocking
  • Floor cleaning with cross-contamination controls between clinical and non-clinical spaces
  • Trash and biohazard-adjacent surface handling (not regulated medical waste disposal itself — that stays with your licensed waste hauler)

What's Usually NOT Included

Most standard medical cleaning contracts exclude regulated medical waste disposal, sharps container servicing, post-construction cleaning, and specialty deep-cleaning of sterilization equipment. If your practice needs any of these, ask for a supplemental rate schedule in writing before you sign.

Watch for These Contract Clauses

  • Auto-renewal clauses: many contracts renew automatically unless you give written notice inside a defined window.
  • Termination fees: understand what it costs to leave before the term ends.
  • Complaint and remedy procedures: how do you report a missed clean, and what's the response timeline?
  • Insurance requirements: the contract should confirm general liability and workers' compensation coverage, with a certificate available on request.

Always Ask for It in Writing

A written scope of work protects you if service quality ever falls short, and it gives you a clear basis for evaluating a provider's performance over time. Any provider unwilling to put the details in writing for a medical facility is a provider to avoid. See our process page for how we structure a written scope before work begins.
